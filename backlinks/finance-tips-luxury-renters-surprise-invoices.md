**Title:** Anyone else get hit by the "second invoice" on a luxury charter? Quote vs. final bill gap

In March 2022 I chartered a 62-foot yacht out of Fort Lauderdale for a long weekend. The quote was $18,500. The final statement came to $26,240. Nothing was hidden, exactly. It was all in the contract, just not in the headline number: fuel, a suggested 20 percent gratuity, dockage, and a provisioning bill I'd waved through without thinking.

Since then I treat every luxury rental, whether jet, villa or yacht, as having two invoices. The first is the quote. The second is everything that settles afterward. Before I sign, I now ask for the second one in writing. How is fuel measured and billed? Is gratuity included or just suggested? What are the dockage, cleaning, deposit and tax lines? Who handles provisioning, and at what markup?

Then I add a 25 to 40 percent buffer to the quote and treat that as the real price. If the total still feels fine, I book. If it doesn't, I've saved myself a bad surprise.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How can I avoid unexpected costs when chartering a yacht or renting a luxury villa or private jet?

The most useful shift is to budget for the second invoice, not the first. The quote is rarely the number you pay.

I learned this in March 2022, chartering a 62-foot yacht out of Fort Lauderdale for a long weekend. The quote was $18,500. The final statement was $26,240. The difference was fuel, a suggested 20 percent gratuity, dockage and a provisioning bill I hadn't scrutinized. All of it was legitimate, and none of it was in the headline price.

Here is the process I use now, whether it's a yacht, villa or jet.

1. Ask what is not included. Request a written list of every cost billed after the base rate. Fuel, taxes, cleaning, crew gratuity, dockage or landing and handling fees, and security deposits are the usual suspects.

2. Pin down how variable costs are measured. Fuel is the big one on boats and jets. Ask whether it's billed at cost or with a markup, and how consumption is calculated.

3. Clarify gratuity. Is it included, or suggested? A suggested 20 percent on a large charter is a real number, so plan for it.

4. Set a provisioning budget up front. Give the broker or owner a cap, or ask for an itemized list before purchases are made.

5. Add a buffer. I add 25 to 40 percent to the quote and treat that as my real price. If the trip still makes sense at that number, I book.

6. Get the final terms in writing before you pay the deposit.

None of this makes a charter cheaper. It makes the cost predictable, which is what most people actually want.
