Your Charter Budget Is Wrong, and the Fix Is Boring

General finance tips from a charter veteran: keep cash liquid, price the full trip, fund luxury with dull money, and read the cancellation schedule.

The best general finance tip I know is also the least glamorous: decide where the money for your next big spend lives before you spend it. Everything else is detail. In March 2022 I wired a $38,000 deposit on a week-long yacht charter out of Split at 6:40 a.m., standing in a Lisbon hotel lobby with a cold espresso, and I never opened the cancellation schedule. A family emergency took the trip off the table in May, and I got back $19,000. Nobody cheated me. I just hadn't read page four.

That lesson cost me more than any bad investment I've made, and I've made a few. Since then I've spent a lot of time with people who charter jets, rent villas for a month at a time and book yachts for the Med season. The ones who stay comfortable aren't the ones with the biggest incomes. They're the ones with dull systems.

Liquidity beats returns when a deposit is due

Cash feels like a wasted asset until a $60,000 deposit lands on a Friday. The Federal Reserve's household well-being survey found that about 63 percent of U.S. adults in 2023 could cover a $400 emergency using cash or its equivalent. That means more than a third couldn't. Wealthier households do better, but I've watched plenty of high earners scramble because their money sat in a brokerage account they didn't want to sell in a down month.

My rule is simple. Keep six months of fixed costs in a high-yield account, plus a separate sinking fund for travel. Not the same bucket. When the two blur together, a good trip quietly eats your safety net.

Price the whole trip, not the headline rate

Quoted rates lie by omission. On a yacht, the advance provisioning allowance usually adds somewhere between 25 and 35 percent on top of the base fee, and nobody mentions it until the second email. On a jet, charter flights are generally subject to a 7.5 percent federal excise tax, which you can confirm through the IRS, and that's before landing fees, catering and the empty return leg. Villas add cleaning fees, damage holds and local tourist taxes.

So I build every budget backward. Take the number the broker gives you, add 40 percent, and see whether you still want it. If the answer's yes, you're fine. If it's a wince, you've saved yourself a bad week.

The empty leg trap

Empty-leg deals look like bargains. Sometimes they are. But your schedule bends around the aircraft, not the other way, and I've seen a "40 percent discount" disappear once a hotel night and a rebooked connection got added. Fund luxury with boring money

Here's my opinionated take: if you're paying for charters out of a portfolio you actively trade, you're making two bets at once. The data isn't kind to stock pickers either. S&P's SPIVA scorecards have shown for years that close to 90 percent of active U.S. large-cap funds trail the S&P 500 over 15 years. Low-cost index funds won't make a good story at dinner. They will make the dinner possible.

I covered the mindset side of this in why most rich people still get finance wrong, and the short version is that status spending and wealth building pull in opposite directions. Pick which one you're doing each year.

Okay, a slightly embarrassing confession. A couple of years ago I decided I was clever enough to time a currency swing before paying a villa owner in euros. My friend Dario told me not to. I didn't listen. The euro went up 3 percent in nine days and I paid about $2,100 extra for the privilege of feeling smart... I'm still not sure if there's a good way to do this, honestly, but I now just pay the invoice the day it arrives.

Treat the paper trail as part of the asset

Contracts are where money actually gets kept or lost. Before you send any deposit, check these:

  • The cancellation schedule, with dates, not just percentages
  • Whether the deposit sits in escrow or goes straight to the operator
  • Who's liable for weather, mechanical failure and substitution of the vessel or aircraft
  • Whether you paid by card, which gives you chargeback options a wire never will

Wires are irreversible. I use them only for deposits above card limits, and only after the escrow question has a written answer.

This is also where renting often beats owning on pure risk. If you haven't read the finance rules that hold up when you rent the jet instead of buying the hangar, start there, because fixed costs on owned assets keep running whether you fly or not. That said, renting isn't free of traps. Heavy frequent use can tip the math back toward fractional ownership, and the break-even point varies a lot by operator. For more on this, see Your Charter Budget Is Lying to You About Real Costs.

Tonight, open the contract for the next trip you've booked and find the cancellation schedule. Write the date of the first penalty on your calendar, then move that deposit amount into its own labeled account. It takes ten minutes.

Frequently asked questions

How much cash should I keep set aside for luxury travel?

Keep a separate travel fund equal to your next planned deposit plus 40 percent for extras like provisioning, taxes and fees. Hold it apart from your six-month emergency reserve so one doesn't drain the other.

Is it smarter to charter or buy a yacht or jet?

For most people who travel fewer than a few dozen days a year, chartering costs less once you count crew, storage, insurance and depreciation. Heavy, predictable use can change that, so run your own break-even before deciding.

Should I pay a charter deposit by wire or credit card?

Use a card when the limit allows, because it gives you chargeback rights. Wires can't be recalled, so use them only when the deposit is large and the funds go into a confirmed escrow account.
