Rent the Jet, Skip the Hangar: Finance Rules That Hold Up

Smart finance tips for people who spend on jets, villas and yachts: why renting beats owning, where the money leaks, and one rule to apply today.

The best general finance tip I know is also the most boring: spend on the experience, not on the asset that carries it. Rent the villa, charter the yacht, and let someone else carry the depreciation. I learned this the expensive way in March 2019, when I stood on a dock in Fort Lauderdale holding a broker's quote for a 52-foot motor yacht, roughly $740,000, and realized the annual running costs on the spreadsheet came to about $78,000 before I had left the marina once.

I didn't buy it. I chartered the same class of boat for nine days that summer for around $41,000, fuel included, and then I went home with the cash intact. That gap between owning and renting is where most luxury spenders quietly bleed, and it's the same gap that wrecks ordinary budgets, just with fewer zeros. If you want the wider version of that argument, I covered it in why most rich people still get finance wrong.

Why the Sticker Price Is the Smallest Number

The rule of thumb among yacht brokers is that annual upkeep runs about 10 percent of the vessel's value. Crew, dockage, insurance, haul-outs, a surprise generator failure in Antibes. My own numbers landed a little under that, which tells you the rule is a floor for older boats and a ceiling for new ones. Aircraft are worse, because fixed costs like hangar space, pilot training and inspections arrive whether or not the plane ever leaves the ground.

So the first move is to price the cost per use, not the cost per asset. Divide every annual expense by the days you'd actually be aboard, in the house, or in the cabin. I've watched a friend's villa in Tulum work out to about $2,300 a night, once taxes, staff and the roof repair were counted. He could have rented a better place for $1,400.

Owning isn't always wrong. If you'll use a property more than 120 days a year, the math often flips. Under that, it rarely does.

The Emergency Fund Nobody Funds

People who charter private jets have, in my experience, the same blind spot as everyone else: no liquid cushion. The Federal Reserve's Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households report found that only about 63 percent of adults could cover a $400 emergency using cash or its equivalent. That's the general public. I've met people with seven-figure incomes who'd fail a scaled-up version of the same test, because their cash was parked in a boat.

Set the number by lifestyle, not by salary

Six months of expenses is the standard advice. For anyone with leased aircraft time, a villa mortgage or crew on payroll, I'd push it to nine. Fixed obligations don't shrink when the market does.

Keep it boring

A high-yield savings account or Treasury bills. Nothing clever. And yes, it will feel wasteful when everything else is up.

Investor Behavior Costs More Than Fees

Fees get the headlines, but timing does more damage. Morningstar's Mind the Gap research has repeatedly shown that investors earn less than the funds they own, by something like a full percentage point a year, because they buy after rallies and sell after drops. Compounded over 20 years, that's a very expensive habit.

I've seen this pattern with clients who book a month on the Amalfi coast the same week they panic-sold a position. The trip wasn't the problem. The sale was.

Here's the part I genuinely wrestle with. Automating everything sounds right, but I'm not 100% sure it works for people whose income swings wildly, like deal-based earners, since a fixed monthly transfer can starve the account in a lean quarter. For them, a percentage-of-income rule probably beats a fixed amount. I haven't found a clean answer.

A Short System for Spending Without Guilt

Okay, a confession. Two summers ago I put a $9,000 villa deposit on the wrong card, a points card with a 29 percent rate, because I was typing on my phone at a dentist's office and wanted it done. I didn't catch it for five weeks. My friend Dario still brings it up at dinner, and honestly, he's right to.

The fix was a system, not willpower. Here's what I use now:

  • One card for deposits and holds, paid in full on the statement date.
  • A separate sinking fund for travel, topped up monthly, so a charter never touches your investments.
  • A 48-hour rule on any booking over $10,000 that you haven't planned.

None of it is exciting. But it's the reason I can say yes to a good week on the water without flinching.

Pick one trip or property you're tempted to buy, and tonight divide its full annual cost by the days you'd realistically use it. If the nightly figure beats a comparable rental by less than 20 percent, rent. Then move the difference into a separate account before you book anything.

Frequently asked questions

Is it cheaper to charter a yacht than to own one?

For most people, yes. Ownership typically costs around 10 percent of the boat's value each year before you leave the dock, so unless you use it well over 100 days a year, chartering wins on cost per day.

How big should an emergency fund be if I have a lot of fixed luxury expenses?

Aim for nine months of total expenses rather than the usual six. Mortgages, crew wages, hangar fees and insurance keep coming due when income drops.

What's the simplest way to keep travel spending from hurting my investments?

Open a dedicated travel account and fund it monthly. Pay for trips only from that account, so a charter or villa week never forces you to sell an investment.
