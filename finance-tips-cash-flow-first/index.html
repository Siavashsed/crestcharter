Your Net Worth Isn't the Problem. Your Cash Flow Is

Sound finance tips for people who spend on jets, villas and yachts: fix cash flow first, rent before you buy, and stop chasing returns.

The best finance tip I know is boring: know what you spend each month before you decide what you can afford to own. In March 2022 I put a deposit on a 62-foot yacht in Fort Lauderdale. I'd read the brochure twice and the maintenance schedule never. Eleven months later the haul-out, crew, insurance and dockage had cost me $214,000 against a purchase I'd budgeted at about $90,000 a year to run. The boat was lovely. The spreadsheet was a horror show.

That mistake wasn't about money I lacked. It was about money I'd never tracked. Most of the people I meet in luxury travel and property have a decent net worth and no real picture of where it leaks, and that gap is where bad decisions live.

Cash flow beats net worth every time

Net worth is a snapshot. Cash flow is the movie. You can hold $8 million in assets and still be forced to sell something at a bad moment because the bills arrive monthly and the sale proceeds don't.

Even among ordinary households the buffer is thin. The Federal Reserve found in its 2023 household well-being survey that around 37% of adults couldn't cover a $400 emergency with cash or its equivalent. Wealthier households do better, sure. But a $400 emergency on a yacht is a $40,000 emergency, so the ratio of surprise to cushion looks similar.

So here's the rule I use now. Hold six to twelve months of total carrying costs in cash, not six months of living expenses. Carrying costs means every toy, every villa, every membership you'd keep paying even if your income stopped tomorrow.

Rent first, and make the asset prove itself

I'm biased here, and I'll say so. After the yacht, I chartered for three straight seasons before touching another purchase. It cost me roughly $310,000 over that period, which stung, and still came in well under what ownership would have run. I wrote more on that logic in the finance rules for renting the jet and skipping the hangar, and I'd stand behind every line of it.

Renting gives you something ownership can't: a trial. You find out whether you actually use a villa 40 days a year or 9. Most people guess high. In my experience the real number runs about a third of the forecast.

When owning does make sense

It's not a universal rule. If you'll use an asset more than around 150 days a year, or it appreciates in a way charter can't match (some waterfront land does), the math flips. But treat that as the exception you have to argue your way into.

The returns trap nobody budgets for

Here's a number that should bother you. Morningstar's Mind the Gap study found that investors earned roughly a full percentage point less per year than the funds they held over a ten-year stretch, mostly because they bought after rallies and sold after drops. A single point, compounded on a large portfolio, is the price of a very nice villa.

I've watched this happen to clients who were otherwise careful. They'd sit through a 15% dip, then panic at 22%. The fix isn't willpower, it's structure. Automate contributions, set rebalancing dates on the calendar, and make selling require a 48-hour wait and a written reason.

If you want the longer argument about why wealthy people stumble on exactly these basics, I laid it out in why most rich people still get finance wrong.

Small systems that actually hold up

Honest aside, and I'm not 100% sure this works for everyone. My friend Dario swears by a separate checking account called "Stuff I Regret," where he moves 5% of every windfall and pretends it's gone. I laughed at him. Then last spring I caught myself booking a $6,800 weekend in Lake Como at 1 a.m. after a glass of wine too many, and I quietly opened my own version the next morning. Don't judge me... it's been surprisingly effective. Whether that's discipline or just a good trick, I couldn't tell you.

Beyond the silly account, these are the habits I'd keep:

  • Review every recurring charge quarterly. I found $11,400 a year in memberships I'd forgotten.
  • Separate the toy budget from the investing budget. Never let one borrow from the other.
  • Get insurance quotes annually. Premiums on high-value assets drift up quietly.
  • Write down the exit cost before you buy anything with a hull, a wing or a mortgage.

None of this is glamorous. But the people I know who stay wealthy for decades are almost always the ones doing the dull version of it.

Tonight, pull the last twelve months of statements and add up every cost tied to the things you own or rent for pleasure. Compare that total to your cash reserves. If the reserves cover less than six months of it, fix that before you spend another dollar on anything shiny.

Frequently asked questions

How much cash should I keep if I own luxury assets?

Aim for six to twelve months of total carrying costs, not just living expenses. That includes insurance, storage, crew, taxes and financing on every asset you'd keep paying for if income stopped.

Is it cheaper to charter than to own a yacht or jet?

Usually, if you'd use the asset fewer than roughly 100 to 150 days a year. Ownership starts to win only with heavy use or an asset that holds its value well.

Why do investors earn less than the funds they own?

They tend to buy after prices climb and sell after they fall. Morningstar's research puts the drag at around one percentage point a year over a decade, and automating contributions is the simplest way to reduce it.
