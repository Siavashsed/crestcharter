**Title:** A $38k deposit dispute taught me why cash on hand beats any investment pick. What's your buffer rule?

In March 2022 I was standing in a marina office in Fort Lauderdale when a 78-foot charter I'd booked for a client fell through. The $38,000 deposit ended up in dispute, and my operating account had $11,400 in it. No investment pick I'd ever made mattered in that moment. Only liquidity did.

Since then I've held to two boring rules. First, keep more cash than feels sensible. Not the 'three months of expenses' number you read everywhere, but enough that a single disputed payment, a delayed refund or a vendor chargeback doesn't force you to sell anything or borrow at a bad rate. Second, pay off anything charging double digits before investing a dollar. A guaranteed 20+% return from clearing a card beats almost any market bet, and it carries no risk.

The uncomfortable part is that a big cash balance looks like wasted opportunity right up until the day it isn't. Inflation drag is real, but it's a small, known cost compared to a forced sale at the wrong time.

Curious how others size theirs, especially anyone with lumpy or irregular income. Do you use a fixed number of months, or a dollar figure tied to your largest single exposure?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What is the most important general finance tip to follow before you start investing?

The most important tip is also the least exciting: build a cash buffer larger than feels comfortable, and pay off high-interest debt before you invest anything.

Here's why this order matters.

**1. Debt at double-digit rates is a guaranteed loss.** If a credit card charges you 22% a year, paying it down is the equivalent of earning a risk-free 22% return. No fund, stock or property reliably beats that, and none comes without volatility. Clear anything above roughly 8 to 10% before you put money in the market.

**2. Cash is what keeps you from selling at the wrong time.** Investments are only good long-term holdings if you never have to liquidate them on someone else's schedule. Without a buffer, a job loss, medical bill or disputed payment forces you to sell during a downturn or borrow at a high rate. That turns a temporary dip into a permanent loss.

**3. Size the buffer to your real exposure.** The standard advice is three to six months of expenses. That's a fair starting point for a salaried person. If your income is irregular, you run a business, or you carry large one-off commitments, base it on your biggest single risk. I learned this when a major deposit of $38,000 went into dispute while my operating account held about $11,400. Having a larger reserve would have meant calm decisions instead of scrambling.

**4. Accept the cost.** Idle cash loses a little to inflation. That cost is small, visible and bounded. A forced sale or a high-interest loan is neither.

**5. Keep it boring.** Use a high-yield savings account or a money market fund, separate from your everyday account, so you aren't tempted to spend it.

Once the buffer is full and the expensive debt is gone, investing becomes far easier, because you can hold through volatility without fear. I wrote up the full story and the numbers here if you want more detail: https://siavashsed.github.io/crestcharter/general-finance-tips-cash-buffer-first/
