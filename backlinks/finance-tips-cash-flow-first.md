**Title:** Has anyone been blindsided by the running costs of a big asset they could technically afford?

In March 2022 I put a deposit on a 62-foot yacht in Fort Lauderdale. I'd read the brochure twice and the maintenance schedule never. I had budgeted about $90,000 a year to run it. Eleven months later, haul-out, crew, insurance and dockage had cost me $214,000.

The purchase price wasn't what hurt. On paper I could afford to own it. What I never did was map my monthly cash outflow before committing. Net worth said I was fine. Cash flow told the truth.

Here's what I do now before buying anything big, whether it's a boat, a second home or a share in an aircraft:

1. List every recurring cost, then assume the ones I've never paid before are double.
2. Ask what the monthly number is, not the sticker price.
3. Read the maintenance schedule first and the brochure second.
4. Hold a cash reserve equal to at least a year of running costs.

I'm curious whether others have been caught out the same way. What was your version of this, and what would you check first next time?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How can I tell whether I can really afford to own an expensive asset like a yacht, second home or private aircraft?

Don't start with net worth. Start with monthly cash flow.

Most people check whether they can cover the purchase price, see that they can, and stop there. That's the mistake. The purchase is a single event. Ownership is a stream of costs that never stops, and it usually runs higher than the brochure suggests.

I learned this on a 62-foot yacht in Fort Lauderdale. I put down a deposit in March 2022 having read the brochure twice and the maintenance schedule never. I had budgeted about $90,000 a year to run it. Eleven months in, haul-out, crew, insurance and dockage had cost $214,000. Nothing was mismanaged. I had simply never priced the real running costs before I committed.

Here's the process I'd use now:

1. Work out what you actually spend each month today. Not what you think you spend, but what your statements show.
2. List every recurring cost of the asset: insurance, storage or dockage, staff, scheduled maintenance, repositioning and depreciation.
3. Read the maintenance schedule before the sales material. It tells you what the asset will demand over its life.
4. Add a margin. If a cost is new to you, assume it will come in well above the estimate in year one.
5. Hold a reserve covering at least twelve months of running costs, separate from the money you used for the purchase.

If the monthly number fits comfortably inside your existing cash flow, you can afford to own it. If it only works by drawing down capital, you can afford to buy it but not to keep it.
