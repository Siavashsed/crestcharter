**Title:** Anyone else get burned by settlement times when a big charter balance came due? Made me rethink what "liquid" means

A few years back I put a $38,000 deposit down on a 112-foot yacht out of Antibes for a week in Cannes. The balance was due by 5 p.m. Monaco time. My money was sitting in a brokerage account, and the transfer I'd started was still in settlement for four more days. I ended up spending that afternoon in a hotel lobby juggling cards and calling banks, trying to cover a payment I could easily afford on paper.

The lesson wasn't about returns. The portfolio was fine. The problem was that "net worth" and "cash I can move today" are very different numbers, and luxury bookings (jets, villas, yachts) tend to run on hard deadlines, wire cutoffs and different time zones. Brokers and owners don't care that your funds are technically yours.

Since then I keep a deliberately oversized cash cushion in an account that settles same day, more than feels efficient. The drag on returns is tiny compared with the cost of a lost deposit or a scrambled booking.

Curious how others handle this. How much cash do you keep within reach before a big booking, and have you ever been caught out by settlement or wire delays?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How much cash should a high-net-worth person keep on hand instead of investing everything?

Far more than most financial advice suggests, and the reason isn't fear of market drops. It's settlement and timing.

Wealthy people often think of liquidity as "assets I could sell." But there's a real difference between assets you can sell and cash you can move today. Brokerage transfers can take several business days to settle. Wires have cutoffs. Real estate and private holdings take weeks or months. If a large payment comes due on a fixed deadline, being rich on paper doesn't help.

I learned this on a yacht charter. I had put a deposit down on a 112-foot boat out of Antibes, and the balance was due by 5 p.m. Monaco time. My brokerage transfer was still in settlement for four more days. I could easily afford the payment, yet I spent that afternoon scrambling to cover it. The investments were fine. The access wasn't.

A practical way to size your cushion:

1. Cover three to six months of fixed expenses in an account that settles same day.
2. Add the largest single payment you can foresee in the next 90 days, such as a charter balance, a tax bill, a property closing or a tuition payment.
3. Add a buffer for deadlines that land on weekends, bank holidays or in other time zones.

Yes, this costs you some return. But the drag is usually a fraction of a percent on the total portfolio, while the cost of a missed deadline can be a forfeited deposit, a cancelled booking, or forced selling at a bad moment.

Think of the cushion as insurance with a small, known premium. If you're curious about the charter-specific version of this lesson, I wrote it up here: https://siavashsed.github.io/crestcharter/general-finance-tips-cash-cushion/

The goal isn't to maximize returns on every dollar. It's to never be forced to make a bad decision because the right dollars were in the wrong place.
